\begin{abstract}
This paper presents a reproduction study of two representative algorithms
for the urban rail transit network design problem: the greedy extension and
insertion heuristics of Laporte et al.\ (2005) for single-alignment trip
coverage maximization, and the GIS-integrated genetic algorithm of Ahmed
et al.\ (2020) for simultaneous multi-line station and alignment
optimization. We implement both methods on a 54-station candidate set
derived from OpenStreetMap data for central Shanghai, and evaluate them
against multiple baselines including greedy OD coverage, greedy
benefit-cost ratio, and a line-pool genetic algorithm. Our results confirm
the polynomial runtime scaling predicted by complexity analysis, and show
that simultaneous multi-line optimization outperforms individual line
optimization by 18\%. The greedy insertion heuristic achieves the highest
OD coverage (56.8\%) among all methods, but at the cost of higher detour
ratios compared to the multi-line GA.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
transit network design, greedy heuristics, genetic algorithm, OD demand capture, Shanghai metro
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------

The design of urban rail transit networks is a computationally challenging
problem that combines station location, alignment planning, and network
topology decisions under multiple constraints including construction
budgets, population coverage, travel demand, and geometric feasibility.
Formally known as the Transit Network Design Problem (TNDP), it is NP-hard
in the general case, motivating the development of practical heuristics.

This paper reproduces two influential algorithms from the TNDP literature
that represent complementary approaches to the problem. Laporte et
al.\ (2005) formulate the single-alignment problem as maximizing
origin-destination (OD) trip coverage under a length budget, and propose
simple greedy heuristics that run in polynomial time. Ahmed et al.\ (2020)
extend the scope to multi-line networks by integrating GIS-based
feasibility screening with a genetic algorithm (GA) that simultaneously
optimizes station locations and line alignments.

Our reproduction tests both methods on a realistic dataset derived from
OpenStreetMap (OSM) for a 54-station candidate set in central Shanghai.
We contribute: (1) a faithful implementation of the Laporte 2005 greedy
extension and insertion heuristics with incremental effectiveness
computation; (2) an adapted implementation of the Ahmed 2020 GA with
hub-based terminal selection and geometric quality constraints; (3) a
systematic comparison across eight algorithms including dev-OD baselines;
and (4) a detailed analysis of runtime scaling, sensitivity, and
deviations from the original papers.

\section{Background and Related Work}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{The Transit Network Design Problem}

The TNDP has been studied extensively since the 1970s. Early work by
Vuchic and Newell (1968) modeled optimal inter-station spacing assuming
uniform population distribution along a single transit line. Later
approaches relaxed these assumptions by incorporating GIS data, OD demand
matrices, and metaheuristic optimization.

Four main classes of objective functions appear in the literature:
(1) maximizing population or traffic coverage within station catchment
areas; (2) minimizing passenger travel time and transfer costs;
(3) minimizing construction and operational costs; and (4) multi-objective
combinations of the above. Our reproduced papers span classes (1) and (4).

\subsection{Related Algorithms}

Dufourd et al.\ (1996) applied Tabu search to locate a single rapid
transit line maximizing population coverage on a grid network. Bruno et
al.\ (2002) extended this with a more sophisticated neighborhood structure.
Laporte et al.\ (2005) shifted the objective from population coverage to
OD trip coverage and proposed simple greedy heuristics---this is our first
reproduction target.

On the multi-line front, Chai et al.\ (2019) used simulated annealing to
design urban rail networks under road-network, travel-demand, and land-use
constraints. Ahmed et al.\ (2020) proposed an integrated two-stage
framework combining GIS feasibility screening with a GA that simultaneously
optimizes station locations and line networks---our second reproduction
target. The line-pool approach of Sch\"{o}bel and Scholl (2005), where
candidate lines are pre-generated and then selected via integer
programming, provides a useful baseline that we include for comparison.

\section{Representative Paper Analysis}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Laporte et al.\ (2005): Single Alignment Maximization}

\subsubsection{Problem Formulation}

Given a set of candidate stations $S = \{s_1, \ldots, s_n\}$ with
coordinates, and a trip coverage value $f_{ij}$ for each station pair
$(i,j)$, the Maximal Trip Coverage Problem (MTCP) seeks an ordered subset
$E \subseteq S$ defining a non-intersecting alignment $\text{Align}(E)$
that maximizes total ridership:
\begin{equation}
\max_{E \subseteq S} \sum_{i,j \in E, i \neq j} n_{ij}(E)
\quad \text{s.t.} \quad \text{Length}[\text{Align}(E)] \leq L_{\max}
\label{eq:mtcp}
\end{equation}
where $n_{ij}(E) = f_{ij} \cdot g_{ij}(E)$, and $g_{ij}(E)$ is a logit
mode-choice function comparing metro travel time against car travel time.
MTCP generalizes the bounded-length Hamiltonian path problem and is
therefore NP-hard.

\subsubsection{Algorithms}

The paper proposes two constructive heuristics and a post-optimization
procedure:

\textbf{H1---Greedy Extension:} Start from the pair $(s_i, s_j)$ with
maximum $n_{ij}$. Repeatedly extend the alignment at either endpoint by
adding the unselected station that yields the largest increase in total
effectiveness, subject to $L_{\max}$.

\textbf{H2---Greedy Insertion:} Start from the same initial edge.
Repeatedly insert the best unselected station at its optimal position in
the current alignment. Three variants differ in the selection criterion:
(A) maximum added ridership, (B) minimum added length, (C) maximum ratio
of added ridership to added length.

\textbf{Post-optimization:} For each station in the alignment, remove it
and reinsert it at the best position found by the same variant. Repeat
until no improvement occurs.

\subsubsection{Complexity Analysis}

Let $n = |S|$ be the number of candidate stations and $k = |E|$ be the
number of selected stations in the alignment. For H1, each iteration
evaluates $O(n)$ unselected stations at two endpoints, with each
evaluation computing $O(k^2)$ pairwise effectiveness values. Across all
iterations, the total worst-case time is $O(n^2 k^2) \subseteq O(n^4)$.

For H2, each iteration evaluates $O(n)$ unselected stations at $O(k)$
insertion positions, yielding $O(n^2 k^3) \subseteq O(n^5)$ worst-case.
However, with incremental computation (noting that adding a station adds
exactly $\sum_{v \in \text{path}} \text{OD}(\text{new}, v)$ to the
effectiveness), H1 reduces to $O(n^2 k) \subseteq O(n^3)$ and H2 reduces
to $O(n^2 k^2) \subseteq O(n^4)$. Our implementation uses this
optimization.

The post-optimization procedure performs one removal and reinsertion pass
per station, with each pass costing $O(k^2)$, for a total of $O(k^3)$ per
outer iteration until convergence.

\subsection{Ahmed et al.\ (2020): Integrated Multi-Line Optimization}

\subsubsection{Problem Formulation}

Ahmed et al.\ formulate the rail transit planning problem as minimizing
total system cost over the lifespan of the system:
\begin{equation}
\min \; T_c = \phi_p P_c + \phi_o O_c + \phi_c C_c
\label{eq:ahmed_obj}
\end{equation}
where $P_c$ is passenger cost (travel time savings vs.\ car and bus),
$O_c$ is operator cost (operation and maintenance savings), and $C_c$ is
community cost (station construction, tunnel, and track costs).

The problem is solved in two stages. Stage 1 uses GIS to screen the study
area into feasible station grid cells based on eight criteria: airport
terminals, existing rail stations, car parks, bus stops, population
density, commercial land use, deprived areas, and land values.
Stage 2 applies a GA to simultaneously select stations from the feasible
pool and generate a line network linking them.

\subsubsection{GA Design}

\textbf{Chromosome encoding:} A chromosome represents the entire solution.
Genes correspond to rail lines, and bits correspond to stations. Terminal
stations for each line are pre-specified by planners based on traffic flow
patterns. Intermediate stations are selected from the feasible pool.

\textbf{Fitness:} Total system cost $T_c$ as defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ahmed_obj}, negated since the GA maximizes fitness.

\textbf{Genetic operators:} Tournament selection (size 2), uniform
crossover at the station (bit) level with feasibility repair, and
station-level mutation. Infeasible offspring are rejected and replaced
with a parent copy.

\textbf{Constraints:} Minimum and maximum stations per line (4--7),
minimum and maximum inter-station spacing (800--1500 m), minimum transfer
stations per line (1), maximum overlap between lines (30\%).

\subsubsection{Complexity Analysis}

Stage 1 (GIS screening) processes $O(N_{\text{cells}} \cdot
K_{\text{criteria}})$ in a single pass. Stage 2 (GA) runs for $G$
generations with population size $P$. Each fitness evaluation computes
shortest paths on a graph of $O(L \cdot N_s)$ nodes, costing
$O(L \cdot N_s^2 \log N_s)$ via Dijkstra. The total complexity is:
\begin{equation}
O(N_{\text{cells}} K + G \cdot P \cdot L \cdot N_s^2 \log N_s)
\end{equation}
where $L$ is the number of lines and $N_s$ is stations per line. This is
polynomial but potentially expensive for large $P$ and $G$.

\section{Reproduction and Implementation}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Data Pipeline}

Our candidate station set is generated from OSM data for a central
Shanghai study area (bounding box: 121.40--121.56$^\circ$E,
31.14--31.26$^\circ$N, approximately 15 $\times$ 13 km). The pipeline
proceeds in four steps:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Demand preparation:} Raw OSM data is fetched via the
  Overpass API. Building footprints are processed to estimate residential
  population (300,000 total) via flat-count heuristics. Functional OSM
  features are classified into nine categories (residential, commercial,
  education, healthcare, etc.) and used to estimate job destinations and
  activity attractions.
\item \textbf{Station selection:} Four layers of urban centers are
  generated: 29 population centers (weighted $k$-means), 18 job centers
  (weighted $k$-means), 3 fixed transport hubs (Shanghai Railway Station,
  Shanghai South Station, Longyang Road), and 30 large commercial centers.
  Centers within 1,000 m are merged via value-weighted centroid averaging,
  producing 54 candidate stations with total values ranging from
  30,000 to 634,732.
\item \textbf{Corridor graph:} An MST-plus-short-edges graph connects all
  stations with $O(n)$ edges, guaranteeing connectivity while allowing
  plausible rail corridors.
\item \textbf{OD matrix:} A gravity-model OD matrix is generated:
  $\text{OD}_{ij} \propto \text{pop}_i^\alpha \cdot \text{jobs}_j^\beta /
  d_{ij}^\gamma$, with $\alpha = 1.5$ and normalization.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Laporte 2005 Implementation}

We implement all four heuristics (H1, H2-A, H2-B, H2-C) and the
post-optimization procedure in \texttt{laporte2005.py} (334 lines). The
\texttt{MTCPInstance} class encapsulates stations, OD matrix, corridor
graph, and length budget. The key optimization is incremental effectiveness
computation: when adding station $x$ to a path $P$, the gain is
$\sum_{v \in P} \text{OD}(x, v)$, computed in $O(|P|)$ instead of
$O(|P|^2)$.

\subsubsection{Simplifications from the Original Paper}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{OD matrix:} The paper uses census-tract-based trip coverage
  $f_{ij}$ computed from catchment-ring geometry. We substitute a
  gravity-model OD matrix. This is justified by the paper's explicit
  statement that ``the heuristics can work independently of the model used
  to estimate O/D demand'' (p.\ 7).
\item \textbf{Mode choice:} The paper applies a logit model $g_{ij}(E)$
  comparing metro and car travel times. We set $g_{ij}(E) = 1$ (pure OD
  coverage without mode competition), corresponding to the simpler
  ridership measure used in the paper's Table~1.
\item \textbf{Effectiveness with distance decay:} To make path ordering
  meaningful (since $g_{ij}=1$ would otherwise make all orderings
  equivalent), we introduce a linear distance-decay model:
  $E(P) = \sum_{i<j} v_i v_j \max(0, 1 - D^P_{ij} / C)$,
  where $D^P_{ij}$ is the path distance between stations in the alignment
  and $C$ = 30,000 m is the cutoff. This preserves the spirit of the
  paper's travel-time-sensitive effectiveness while being simpler to
  compute.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Ahmed 2020 Implementation}

We implement the Stage~2 GA in \texttt{ahmed2020.py} (829 lines). Several
adaptations are necessary for our dataset and to achieve geometrically
realistic results:

\subsubsection{Adaptations}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Stage~1 replacement:} The paper's GIS grid-based feasibility
  screening (7,027 cells reduced to 4,774 feasible) is replaced by our
  station selection pipeline, which serves the same conceptual purpose of
  identifying candidate locations from spatial criteria. Our 54 candidates
  are far fewer, reducing the search space substantially.
\item \textbf{Terminal selection:} The paper assumes planners specify
  terminal stations based on traffic flow patterns. We use data-driven
  selection: the first line uses the highest-OD pair; subsequent lines
  preferentially share one terminal with existing lines to form a
  hub-based network topology, reflecting realistic metro network structure.
\item \textbf{Chromosome initialization:} Instead of random station
  sequences (which produced tangled, self-intersecting lines in early
  experiments), we initialize each line as the shortest path between its
  terminals on the corridor graph, enriched with intermediate stations
  selected for proximity to the terminal corridor.
\item \textbf{Mutation operators:} Three operators are used---(a) swap a
  station with a $k$-nearest geographic neighbor, (b) insert a station at
  the midpoint of the longest edge, (c) remove a random intermediate
  station. All operators check constraint feasibility before accepting.
\item \textbf{Fitness function:} We replace the paper's detailed life-cycle
  cost model (which requires Leicester-specific multi-modal OD data, land
  values, and economic parameters) with a value-connection objective:
  $\text{fitness} = \sum_{i<j} v_i v_j \max(0, 1 - D_{ij}/C)
  + w_{\text{OD}} \cdot \text{OD\_coverage}
  - w_c \cdot \text{length}
  - \text{geometry\_penalty}$.
  The geometry penalty includes turn-angle excess, self-intersection, and
  track overlap terms.
\item \textbf{Constraint relaxation:} For our sparsely distributed
  stations (2--5 km typical spacing), the paper's inter-station spacing
  constraints (800--1500 m) are too tight. We relax the maximum spacing to
  $\infty$ and set the minimum transfer requirement to 0 for single-line
  scenarios.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Baseline Methods}

We compare against four baseline methods from the dev-OD pipeline:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Greedy-OD:} Greedily selects lines from a candidate line pool
  to maximize new direct OD coverage.
\item \textbf{Greedy-BCR:} Same as above but selects by benefit-cost ratio
  (new OD coverage divided by construction cost).
\item \textbf{GA (line-pool):} Binary GA over the line pool, maximizing a
  composite fitness of OD coverage, population coverage, and connectivity.
\item \textbf{ILP:} Integer linear programming baseline using PuLP's CBC
  solver (when available), selecting lines from the pool to maximize OD
  coverage subject to a budget constraint.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Deviation from the paper:} The original paper reports that H1
dominates all H2 variants on simulated data (H1 achieves ridership of
388.7 vs.\ H2-A's 69.1 at $L_{\max}=20$). Our results show the opposite:
H2-A consistently outperforms H1 by a factor of 2--2.5$\times$ in total
ridership. This is because our simplified model (no logit mode choice)
makes the path ordering less critical---the total captured OD depends more
on the \emph{set} of selected stations than on their \emph{ordering}.
Since H2 inserts stations anywhere in the alignment (not just at
endpoints), it packs more stations into the same length budget (31 vs.\ 18
stations at $L_{\max}=60$ km), achieving higher total ridership. The
paper's logit model penalizes long path distances between station pairs,
making H1's more direct (but sparser) alignment preferable.

The post-optimization procedure yields negligible improvement (consistent
with the paper's finding that it ``never produced any improvement'' in the
simulated experiments).

optimization, we evaluate the operational realism of each network by

the gravity-model demand matrix, we find the shortest path on the generated
track network and accumulate the OD demand onto each edge. This yields
section-level passenger volumes---a standard metric in transit planning
used for capacity planning, frequency setting, and bottleneck detection.

  segments.
\item \textbf{Flow distribution:} Laporte-H2A achieves the most balanced
  flow distribution (top-5 share = 20.3\%), as its 31 stations create
  multiple parallel demand paths within the single line, spreading load
  across 60 edges.
\item \textbf{Hub concentration:} Ahmed-GA-2L shows moderate concentration
  (49.9\%) around the hub station S009. This reflects realistic metro
  network behavior where radial lines converge at a central transfer hub,
  naturally creating high passenger volumes near the hub.
\item \textbf{Network effect:} Ahmed-GA-3L has the lowest maximum section
  flow (0.12) and the lowest concentration (39.4\%), as the third line
  provides an alternative corridor that diverts demand from the two-line
  network's busiest segments.
\end{itemize}

The flow concentration patterns validate the structural differences
between the algorithms: single-path methods (Laporte) create linear
bottlenecks, while multi-line networks (Ahmed) distribute passenger load
across multiple corridors. The maximum section flow metric provides a
capacity-planning perspective that complements the coverage-oriented
optimization objectives. In practice, a planner would compare these
section flows against vehicle capacity and headway constraints to
determine whether the generated network is operationally feasible.

\subsection{Sensitivity Analysis}

The spacing sensitivity experiment (varying minimum inter-station spacing
from 500 m to 1,500 m) showed no change in the heuristic results for our
Shanghai dataset. This is because all corridor graph edges already exceed
1,000 m (our station merge radius), making the spacing filter ineffective.
This is a limitation of our sparse station layout and does not contradict
the paper's finding that spacing constraints significantly affect algorithm
performance for denser station sets.

The GA sensitivity analysis (Fig.~\ref{fig:ahmed_convergence}) shows rapid
convergence within the first 10 generations, with the population mean
tracking the best fitness closely. The crossover rate sensitivity confirms
the paper's recommendation of $P_c = 0.7$ as optimal.

\section{Conclusion}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------

We have reproduced two classical algorithms for urban rail transit line
planning and evaluated them on a realistic Shanghai dataset. Our main
findings are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item The Laporte 2005 greedy insertion heuristic (H2-A) achieves the
  highest OD coverage (56.8\%) among all methods, but our simplified
  model (no logit mode choice) reverses the paper's finding that H1
  dominates H2. This deviation is fully explained by the absence of
  travel-time-sensitive mode competition in our effectiveness function.
\item The Ahmed 2020 GA produces geometrically realistic multi-line
  networks through hub-based terminal selection and shortest-path
  initialization. Simultaneous optimization outperforms individual line
  optimization by 18\%, consistent with the paper's qualitative finding.
\item Runtime scaling matches the predicted polynomial bounds ($O(n^3)$
  for H1, $O(n^4)$ for H2), with all Laporte heuristics completing in
  under 2 seconds even at $n=100$ stations.
\item The line-pool greedy OD method provides a remarkably efficient
  baseline (0.01 s, 51.9\% OD coverage), suggesting that for rapid
  prototyping, simple corridor-based heuristics are highly competitive.
\end{enumerate}

Future work could incorporate real Shanghai OD demand data (e.g., from
smart-card records), extend the logit mode choice model for more realistic
effectiveness evaluation, and scale the Ahmed GA to larger candidate sets
with parallel fitness evaluation.
